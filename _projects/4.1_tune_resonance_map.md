# Tune Resonance Map

Accelerator physicists keep track of multiple clocks.

In a circular accelerator, the beam undergoes many oscillations around the ring. When the beam reaches its highest energy, the beam will make a revolution around the ring at a near constant speed; accelerator physicists use dipoles to steer the beam and using quadrupoles to focus the beam. The beam then has an associated frequency at which it goes around the ring. This frequency (revolution frequency) can also oscillate slightly depending on the accelerator componenents.

From the magnetic multipole components of the ring (dipoles, quadrupoles, etc.) the particles will also undergo transverse oscillations (perpendicular to direction of travel). These transverse oscillations (betatron oscillations) depend on the magnetic strengths and placements of dipoles and quadrupoles. They are considered in phase space; for one particle's horizontal motion in one spot in the accelerator on the phase space diagram, the particles position $$x$$ and momenta $$p_x$$ move along in an ellipse, where every discrete movement along that ellipse is the particle passing through that spot in the accelerator.

Since the particle's motion is periodic, it will eventually come back to the starting position (or close to it). The frequency of this betatron oscillation per revolution around the ring is called the **tune**. The machine's tune $$Q$$ has an integer part (revolutions around the ring) as well as a fraction part (phase displacement from original phase $$\phi$$), and the fractional portion is denoted as **$$\nu$$**. Since there are horizontal and vertical phase space oscillations, there is a horizontal tune $$\nu_x$$ and a vertical tune $$\nu_y$$.

The beam as a whole will have different tunes throuhgout the distribution, and should vary slighly to remain stable. The "average" tunes that the beam stays at is called the working point, and is represented as the two transverse tunes together. 

A working point is dangerous when it lies on or near a **resonance condition**:

$$m\nu_x ​+ n\nu_y ​= p$$

with integers m, n, p. The order of the resonance is given by $$\|m\| + \|n\|$$. This can be visualized in the interactive plot of the Tune Resonance Map below.

These lines represent dangerous working points because repeated kicks due to lattice errors and multipole fields can lead to an increase in oscillation amplitude. When the working point is close to resonance, the beam goes to the same points in phase space and as a result gets kicked by these same fields and errors every transverse revolution, resulting in an unstable orbit and potential beam loss. When the working point is far (enough) from resonances, the beam doesn't come back to the same points in phase space and experiences multiple kicks in different directions, constantly increasing and decreasing oscillation amplitude and keeping the beam stable.

The analogy that people usually use is a swing. When you push someone with random, incoherent pushes, the swinger stays low to the ground. However, push with the same push at the right resonant frequency, and the swinger goes higher and higher.

Below is an interactive tune map. The working point is marked with a bold **x**, and below the plot shows the resonances it is nearest as well as a "score" for the stability of the working point. This is calculated geometrically from the closest resonance line:

$$d = \frac{\|m_x\nu_x + m_y\nu_y - p\|}{\sqrt{m_x^2 + m_y^2}}$$

The larger the stability score, the more relatively stable it is compared to other working points. The user can click on the resonance orders on the right to make certain resonance orders disappear, as well as set a new working point using the **Set Working Point** button and entering a value between 0 and 1.
